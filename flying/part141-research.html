The XC PIC Question

The most operationally significant difference for low-time pilots: Part 141 eliminates the 50-hour PIC cross-country requirement. Under Part 61, you cannot take your instrument checkride until you have logged 50 hours of PIC XC time. If you are building that time concurrently with instrument training — as most SR22T owners do — this is not a bottleneck. If you have limited flying or are working on the instrument rating early in your pilot career, Part 141's bypass is valuable.

Brett's situation: You started IFR training in June 2026 with an active flight schedule in your SR22T. You're almost certainly accumulating XC PIC time rapidly. Whether 50 hours is a constraint depends on your logbook — worth verifying. If you're close, Part 141's bypass doesn't matter.

04 What Turbine & High-Performance Pilots Say

Pilots in the Pilatus PC-12 owner community, TBM owners, and those transitioning to turbines generally treat the Part 141 vs Part 61 IFR question as irrelevant to their situation.

The Turbine Transition Perspective

For pilots who eventually plan to fly a PC-12, TBM 960, or similar, the instrument rating framework matters less than the quality and depth of the instrument training itself. Turbine transitions happen at Part 142 training centers (FlightSafety, SimCom, CAE) regardless of how you got your IFR ticket.

Key insight from the high-performance community: What matters for turbine readiness is not whether you did Part 141 or Part 61, but whether your IFR training built genuine scan discipline, systems integration, and CRM. Pilots who trained in glass-cockpit aircraft (Cirrus, Lancair, DA62) transition to turbines more smoothly than those who learned on steam gauges.

The Part 142 vs Part 141 Distinction

Many pilots confuse Part 141 and Part 142. These are fundamentally different:

PartWhat It CoversExamplesAircraft?
Part 141 FAA-approved flight schools with aircraft Channel Islands Aviation (KCMA), ATP Flight School, university aviation programs Yes — required in approved TCO
Part 142 Training centers with simulators, no real aircraft required FlightSafety International, SimCom Training, CAE No aircraft — FFS/FTD only
Part 61 Individual instructor-led training Any CFI/CFII, any location Any appropriate aircraft

FlightSafety and SimCom are Part 142 training centers. Their SR22T simulator programs are excellent for recurrent training and type-specific procedures but do not qualify as Part 141 instruction for initial certificate purposes. A SimCom visit can supplement Brett's Part 61 training but does not replace it.

What High-Performance Community Recommends for IFR

  • Train in your actual aircraft — Muscle memory and avionics workflows built in a Cessna must be rebuilt when you get back in your SR22T
  • Find the best instructor, not the best school framework — A mediocre instructor in a Part 141 school is worse than an excellent CSIP under Part 61
  • Consider a SimCom/FSI visit for simulator work — Part 142 centers allow high-rep practice in a risk-free environment; can satisfy up to 20 hrs of Part 61 instrument time
  • The Cirrus Approach IFR program (launched Sep 2025) is the factory-recommended framework — already what Brett is using with Jason Price
  • Stage checks with a different evaluator can be arranged under Part 61 without enrolling in Part 141 — ask Jason Price to include formal stage evaluations

06 The Own-Aircraft Question

Can Brett do Part 141 training in his SR22T G7+ (N269PG)?

Short answer: Almost certainly not, without significant coordination. Part 141 training must be conducted in aircraft listed in the school's FAA-approved Training Course Outline (TCO). Adding a student's personal aircraft to a TCO requires school agreement, FAA application, and approval — this is rarely done for one student.

The Regulatory Reality

14 CFR Part 141.79 requires that training only be conducted when "each airport, aircraft, and facility necessary for that training meets the standards specified in the holder's approved training course outline." CIA's TCO was approved for their Cessna and Beechcraft fleet. Brett's SR22T is not in their TCO.

To use Brett's SR22T in CIA's Part 141 IFR program, CIA would need to:

  • Formally inspect and approve Brett's SR22T against Part 141.39 aircraft requirements
  • Amend their Training Course Outline to include the SR22T (or SR22-category aircraft)
  • Obtain FAA approval for the TCO amendment (can take weeks to months)
  • Have a CFI on staff qualified and current in the SR22T
  • Accept ongoing liability for training in a student-owned aircraft

This is not impossible in theory, but no practical school will do this for one student. The economics don't work for the school, and the FAA process is not trivial.

CIA's "Customer Aircraft" Policy

CIA's website states they "provide training in our fleet as well as customer aircraft." This applies to their Part 61 programs, not their Part 141 programs. Under Part 61, any approved CFI can train in any appropriate aircraft — CIA's instructors can fly with Brett in his SR22T, but that instruction counts as Part 61, not Part 141.
